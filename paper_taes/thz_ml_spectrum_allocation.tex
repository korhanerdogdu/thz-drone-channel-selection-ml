\begin{abstract}
Terahertz (THz) links between unmanned aerial vehicles (UAVs) offer very wide bandwidth,
but molecular absorption splits the band into frequency-dependent transmission windows,
and mobility misaligns the narrow beams. Which frequency channels to activate, and how
to share the transmit power among them, therefore decides the achievable capacity. An
existing threshold-search method, MaxActive, returns the best channel set within its
search family but must repeat its search over thousands of channels at every geometry
update. This correspondence investigates machine-learning alternatives that work from the
same physical channel state. Eighteen supervised regressors from six model families
predict the capacity that the search would deliver, and a dueling double deep
$Q$-network, formulated as a single-step contextual bandit, learns the channel-selection
decision itself. All methods are evaluated with data regenerated from a
physics-consistent THz model under four mobility patterns, using leakage-safe nested
cross-validation. A Gaussian process predicts the capacity most accurately, whereas
single trees, plain boosting, and unregularized linear regression overshoot the physical
bound. The learned policy attains at least 99.7\% of the reference capacity at every
evaluated geometry, but it is trained separately for each operating context, with the
reference capacity in its reward. Once trained, both learned branches lower the online
decision cost by about two orders of magnitude or more and occupy kilobytes of memory.
\end{abstract}

Beam misalignment, channel selection, contextual bandit, deep reinforcement learning,

\section{INTRODUCTION}
\label{sec:intro}
% ============================================================================
T{\scshape erahertz} (THz) frequencies ($0.1$--$10$~THz) offer contiguous bandwidths of
tens to hundreds of gigahertz, which makes them a candidate band for sixth-generation
(6G) wireless systems and, in particular, for high-capacity links between unmanned
aerial vehicles (UAVs) such as drone swarms and aerial backhaul
\cite{akyildiz2022thz,chaccour2022seven,azari2022thzuav}. At the altitudes where drones
operate, the water-vapor concentration is lower than at sea level, so THz links are more
practical in the air than on the ground \cite{saeed2020altitudes,saeed2021cfb}. THz propagation,
however, differs from that at microwave and millimeter-wave frequencies in three ways that
matter for system design. First, the free-space spreading loss grows with the square of
both distance and frequency \cite{jornet2011channel}. Second, water-vapor molecules
absorb energy at many discrete resonance lines, so the attenuation changes by orders of
magnitude across the band; the usable spectrum consists of transmission windows whose
width shrinks as the distance grows \cite{jornet2011channel,han2016multiwideband}.
Third, the absorbed energy is re-radiated as \emph{molecular absorption noise}, so the
noise level also depends on frequency and distance \cite{jornet2011channel}. The link
budget is closed with highly directional antennas, but narrow beams are easily
misaligned when the aircraft move \cite{moorthy2022beam}. As a result, the capacity of a
UAV-to-UAV THz link depends jointly on distance, absorption, noise, antenna gain,
beamwidth, and beam alignment, and it can collapse by many orders of magnitude when the
beams drift apart \cite{saeed2024maxactive}.

\subsection{Spectrum Allocation in the THz Band}
These properties make \emph{spectrum allocation} a central design problem. The band is
divided into many narrow frequency channels, and for every channel geometry the
transmitter must decide which channels to activate and how to divide its fixed transmit
power among them. Activating a channel adds bandwidth, but it also takes power away from
the others. A channel near an absorption line contributes little capacity and much noise,
whereas concentrating the power on too few channels wastes bandwidth. The best choice
changes whenever the distance or the beam alignment changes, so in a mobile UAV link the
decision has to be recomputed continuously.

\subsection{Related Work}
\emph{Optimization-based allocation.} Han \emph{et al.} \cite{han2016multiwideband}
exploited the distance-dependent transmission windows with a multi-wideband waveform and
optimized the sub-band allocation to extend the communication range under rate and power
constraints. Shafie \emph{et al.} \cite{shafie2022subband} designed adaptive sub-band
bandwidths for multiuser THz systems, which reduces the spread of absorption loss across
users. Both target terrestrial links and do not model aerial mobility. For aerial
platforms, Saeed \emph{et al.} \cite{saeed2021cfb} proposed the common flat band (CFB)
scheme, which keeps the channels where path gain and noise spectral density are both
flat, and analyzed the capacity of THz links for drones, aircraft, and satellites. Xu
\emph{et al.} \cite{xu2021thzuav} jointly optimized UAV location, bandwidth, and power,
but for UAV-to-ground links. Beam-oriented works, such as LeTera \cite{moorthy2020letera}
and LeBeam \cite{moorthy2022beam}, adapt the beamwidth to in-flight mobility, but they
assume a single THz channel and therefore do not address channel selection. Saeed
\emph{et al.} \cite{saeed2024maxactive} combined these strands for drone-to-drone links:
they jointly considered channel selection, water-filling (WF) or equal-power (EP)
allocation, and beamwidth adjustment, and proposed the \emph{MaxActive} channel-selection
scheme. MaxActive with EP achieved a capacity close to that of WF-based schemes while
outperforming CFB and standard full-band selection, and it is the reference solution
adopted here. Its main practical drawback is computational. The search re-evaluates the
capacity over the whole band for many candidate channel sets, and the joint allocation
loop must be repeated after every geometry change.

\emph{Learning-based allocation.} Machine learning (ML) has been used to replace costly
resource-allocation algorithms with fast learned mappings. Sun \emph{et al.}
\cite{sun2018learning} showed that a neural network trained to imitate an iterative
power-control algorithm can reproduce its output at a small fraction of its run time,
the ``learning to optimize'' paradigm. Deep reinforcement learning (DRL) has been applied
widely to wireless resource management \cite{luong2019drl}; for example, multi-agent deep
$Q$-learning has been used for dynamic power allocation \cite{nasir2019marl}. For THz
systems, Shafie \emph{et al.} \cite{shafie2022unsupervised} trained a deep network without
labels to approximate a sub-band allocation; as noted in \cite{elcik2025d3qn}, such
schemes are sensitive to training and can occasionally violate hard power or bandwidth
constraints. Hu and Han \cite{hu2025glove}
recently combined graph neural networks with DRL to allocate power and antenna sub-arrays
in dynamic THz UAV networks; their objective is resource efficiency rather than per-link
channel selection. Surveys of ML for THz communications are given in
\cite{farhad2023thzai}. Closest to the present work, Elçik \emph{et al.}
\cite{elcik2025d3qn} formulated THz drone-to-drone channel selection as a contextual
bandit solved by a dueling double deep $Q$-network (D3QN). Their policy matched the
MaxActive capacity with a much lower operation count, but it requires a separately
trained model for every distance--gain pair, and it was tested on a narrower band
($0.5$--$1$~THz) with a single learning method.

\subsection{Research Gap and Contributions}
Two questions remain open. First, is a deep-RL policy necessary, or can simpler
supervised models, trained on the same physical channel state, predict what the
spectrum-allocation search achieves? Second, what do these alternatives cost in
accuracy, physical feasibility, run time, memory, and training effort, under a
validation protocol that is credible for the small datasets a physics simulator
produces? This correspondence addresses both questions for the MaxActive spectrum
allocation problem of \cite{saeed2024maxactive}. The contributions are as follows.
\begin{enumerate}
\def\labelenumi{\arabic{enumi})}
\item A physics-consistent reproduction of the MaxActive capacity under WF and EP
allocation for four UAV mobility patterns, including mobility over elevation and
measured windy-hover acceleration traces. All values are regenerated from the reference
simulation code, not digitized from published figures, and they serve as a reusable
ground truth for learning.
\item A systematic benchmark of eighteen supervised regressors from six model families
that predict the capacity of the spectrum-allocation search from thirteen physical
channel features. The evaluation uses leakage-safe nested cross-validation and reports
how often each model exceeds the physical capacity bound.
\item A D3QN channel-selection policy that operates on the same individual channels as
MaxActive. It is contrasted with the regressors as an example of learning the
allocation \emph{decision} rather than its \emph{value}, and its per-context training
requirement is stated explicitly.
\item A convergence and complexity analysis that combines asymptotic cost with measured
execution time and memory footprint. The analysis separates model inference from the
feature extraction that precedes it, so the reported speed-ups compare like with like.
\end{enumerate}

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. Section~\ref{sec:system} presents
the THz UAV channel model, formulates the spectrum allocation problem, and reviews the
two existing solutions, MaxActive and the D3QN policy. Section~\ref{sec:ml} describes the
alternative ML solutions and how they use the physical channel state.
Section~\ref{sec:perf} gives the simulation parameters, the data generation, and the
results. Section~\ref{sec:complexity} analyzes convergence and complexity, and
Section~\ref{sec:conc} concludes the paper.

\section{SYSTEM MODEL AND BACKGROUND}
\label{sec:system}
% ============================================================================
\subsection{THz UAV Channel Model}
\label{sec:channel}
We consider a link between a transmitting (Tx) and a receiving (Rx) UAV, as in
\cite{saeed2024maxactive}. At time $t$ the two UAVs are at the three-dimensional positions
$\mathbf{p}_{\mathrm{Tx}}(t)$ and $\mathbf{p}_{\mathrm{Rx}}(t)$, separated by the distance
$d=\|\mathbf{p}_{\mathrm{Rx}}(t)-\mathbf{p}_{\mathrm{Tx}}(t)\|$. The time index is dropped
below when no confusion arises. Either UAV may move. In the scenarios studied here, the
Tx hovers and the Rx moves, except for the real-trace scenario, where both move.

\emph{Frequency channels.} The THz band $[f_{\min},f_{\max}]$ is divided into a set
$\mathcal{F}$ of narrowband channels. Channel $i\in\mathcal{F}$ has center frequency $f_i$
and width $\Delta f_i$, which equals the spectral resolution of the atmospheric data. A
spectrum allocation activates a subset $\Omega\subseteq\mathcal{F}$ and assigns a transmit
power $P_i\ge0$ to each active channel.

\emph{Path loss.} The path loss of channel $i$ is the product of a spreading loss and an
absorption loss \cite{jornet2011channel}. The spreading (free-space) loss is the Friis
term, which in decibels, with $d$ in meters and $f_i$ in megahertz, reads
\begin{equation}
L_{\mathrm{spr},i}(d)=20\log_{10}d+20\log_{10}f_i-27.55 .
\label{eq:spread}
\end{equation}
The absorption loss follows from the Beer--Lambert law. The fraction of power that passes
through a path of length $d$ is the transmittance
\begin{equation}
\tau_i(d)=e^{-k_i d},
\label{eq:tau}
\end{equation}
where $k_i$ is the molecular absorption coefficient of the atmosphere at frequency $f_i$.
We obtain $k_i$ from transmittance values computed by the line-by-line radiative transfer
model (LBLRTM) \cite{clough2005aer} for the UAV altitude, as in
\cite{saeed2024maxactive}. The absorption loss is
$L_{\mathrm{abs},i}(d)=10\log_{10}\!\big(1/\tau_i(d)\big)=10\,k_i d\log_{10}e$~dB, and the
total path loss in linear scale is
\begin{equation}
A_i(d)=10^{\,[L_{\mathrm{spr},i}(d)+L_{\mathrm{abs},i}(d)]/10}.
\label{eq:pathloss}
\end{equation}
Because $k_i$ varies by orders of magnitude between neighboring channels, $A_i$ is highly
frequency selective. Moreover, because $L_{\mathrm{abs},i}$ grows linearly with $d$,
the transmission windows narrow as the UAVs move apart.

\emph{Molecular absorption noise.} The molecules that absorb THz energy re-emit it as
noise. The emissivity of the path is $1-\tau_i(d)$, and the noise power in channel $i$ is
\cite{jornet2011channel}
\begin{equation}
P_{N,i}(d)=k_B\,T_0\,\big(1-\tau_i(d)\big)\,\Delta f_i ,
\label{eq:noise}
\end{equation}
where $k_B$ is the Boltzmann constant and $T_0$ is the reference temperature. The noise is
therefore strongest exactly where the absorption is strongest.

\emph{Antenna gain and beam misalignment.} Both UAVs use the three-dimensional
spherical-lobe (sectored) antenna model of \cite{saeed2024maxactive}, with the same
beamwidth $\psi$ (in radians) in elevation and azimuth. The antenna of node
$a\in\{\mathrm{Tx},\mathrm{Rx}\}$ has the gain
\begin{equation}
G_a=\begin{cases}
4\pi/\psi^{2}, & |\Delta\theta_a|\le\psi/2 \text{ and } |\Delta\phi_a|\le\psi/2,\\
\rho, & \text{otherwise},
\end{cases}
\label{eq:gain}
\end{equation}
where $\Delta\theta_a$ and $\Delta\phi_a$ are the azimuth and elevation (zenith) angles
between the antenna boresight and the instantaneous line of sight to the other UAV, and
$\rho\ll1$ is the side-lobe level. When the boresight points to the zenith or the nadir,
the azimuth is undefined and only the elevation condition is applied. The boresights are fixed by the initial geometry. As the UAVs move,
the line of sight rotates away from the boresights, and once it leaves the main lobe the
gain drops from $4\pi/\psi^2$ to $\rho$. This is \emph{beam misalignment}. Widening the
beam (\emph{beamwidth adjustment}) keeps the link in the main lobe, at the price of a
lower gain $4\pi/\psi^2$. The total link gain is $G=G_{\mathrm{Tx}}G_{\mathrm{Rx}}$.

\emph{Per-channel SNR and capacity.} With power $P_i$ on channel $i$, the signal-to-noise
ratio (SNR) of the channel is
\begin{equation}
\gamma_i=\frac{P_i\,G}{A_i(d)\,P_{N,i}(d)} .
\label{eq:snr}
\end{equation}
The SNR thus combines all the physical effects above: distance and frequency through
$A_i$, the atmosphere through $A_i$ and $P_{N,i}$, and geometry and beam alignment through
$G$. Treating each channel as an additive white Gaussian noise channel, the link capacity
of an allocation $(\Omega,\{P_i\})$ is the Shannon sum
\begin{equation}
C(\Omega,\{P_i\})=\sum_{i\in\Omega}\Delta f_i\,\log_2\!\left(1+\gamma_i\right).
\label{eq:capacity}
\end{equation}

\emph{Power allocation.} Two power-allocation policies are considered. Under EP, the total
transmit power $P_T$ is split evenly across the active channels,
\begin{equation}
P_i=P_T/|\Omega|,\qquad i\in\Omega .
\label{eq:ep}
\end{equation}
Under WF, which maximizes \eqref{eq:capacity} for a fixed set $\Omega$
\cite{goldsmith2005},
\begin{equation}
P_i=\max\!\big(0,\;\mu-A_iP_{N,i}/G\big),\qquad i\in\Omega ,
\label{eq:wf}
\end{equation}
where the water level $\mu$ is chosen so that $\sum_{i\in\Omega}P_i=P_T$. WF gives more
power to channels with low attenuation and noise and switches off channels whose
effective noise $A_iP_{N,i}/G$ is above the water level. EP is simpler to implement
because it needs no per-channel power control.

\subsection{Problem Formulation}
\label{sec:problem}
Following \cite{saeed2024maxactive}, the spectrum allocation problem at time $t$ is to
choose the active channel set, the power allocation, and the beamwidth that maximize the
link capacity:
\begin{subequations}
\label{eq:problem}
\begin{align}
\max_{\Omega\subseteq\mathcal{F},\,\{P_i\},\,\psi}\quad & C(\Omega,\{P_i\})
\label{eq:obj}\\
\text{s.t.}\quad & \textstyle\sum_{i\in\Omega}P_i\le P_T,
\label{eq:c1}\\
& P_i\ge0,\ i\in\Omega;\qquad P_i=0,\ i\notin\Omega,
\label{eq:c2}\\
& \psi_0\le\psi\le\psi_{\max},
\label{eq:c3}
\end{align}
\end{subequations}
where $\psi_0$ is the nominal beamwidth and $\psi_{\max}$ the widest admissible one. The
capacity depends on $\psi$ through $G$ in \eqref{eq:snr}. The problem is combinatorial in
$\Omega$, which has $2^{|\mathcal{F}|}$ candidate subsets, and $|\mathcal{F}|$ is in the
thousands in the THz band. It is also coupled, because the best channels depend on the
powers and vice versa. An exhaustive search is therefore infeasible
\cite{saeed2024maxactive}. Problem~\eqref{eq:problem} is solved for every geometry, so its
solution must be recomputed as the UAVs move.

Reference \cite{saeed2024maxactive} decomposes \eqref{eq:problem} into two stages.
\emph{(i)~Beamwidth adjustment:} $\psi$ is chosen first so that the link stays in the
main lobe with the largest gain. \emph{(ii)~Channel selection and power allocation:} for
the resulting gain, a channel-selection rule and the power allocation \eqref{eq:ep} or
\eqref{eq:wf} are iterated until the channel set they produce no longer changes. The
spectrum-allocation \emph{decision} studied in this paper is the output of stage (ii): the
set $\Omega$ and the capacity $C$ that it delivers.

\subsection{Existing Solution: MaxActive}
\label{sec:maxactive}
MaxActive \cite{saeed2024maxactive} solves stage (ii) by a threshold search on the SNR. The
channels are ranked by SNR, and an SNR threshold is lowered from the largest SNR in
decrements $\Delta s$. At each of the $Q$ threshold levels, the candidate set
$\Omega_j$ contains the channels whose SNR exceeds the threshold. The power allocation is
applied to $\Omega_j$, and its capacity \eqref{eq:capacity} is evaluated. MaxActive
returns the candidate set with the largest capacity. Because the candidates are
SNR-ordered prefixes $\Omega_1\subset\Omega_2\subset\cdots$, the search examines at most
$|\mathcal{F}|$ sets rather than $2^{|\mathcal{F}|}$. Each evaluation sums up to
$|\mathcal{F}|$ terms, which gives a per-update cost of $O(Q\,|\mathcal{F}|)$;
\cite{saeed2024maxactive} gives a detailed worst-case operation count. In the joint
scheme, MaxActive and the power allocation run in alternation until their channel sets
agree. Reference \cite{saeed2024maxactive} showed that MaxActive with EP closely
approaches the WF capacity and is the most practical of the schemes it considered.

\emph{In what sense MaxActive is optimal.} The search returns the best member of the
family of SNR-ordered nested subsets, for the given channel state and power allocation.
This optimality has two limits. First, it holds only within the adopted threshold-search
family. Subsets that are not SNR-ordered prefixes are never examined, so global
optimality over all $2^{|\mathcal{F}|}$ subsets is not claimed in general. Under EP with
(near-)equal channel widths, every selected channel receives the same power, and the
capacity-maximizing subset of any given size then consists of the highest-SNR channels.
In that case, restricting the search to SNR-ordered prefixes loses nothing, provided the
search examines every prefix length. Second, selection
and power allocation are optimized alternately rather than jointly, so MaxActive is
optimal \emph{conditioned} on the power allocation supplied to it, not for the joint
selection-and-power problem \eqref{eq:problem}. MaxActive serves in this restricted
sense, within the LBLRTM channel model and the spherical-lobe antenna model, as the
learning target and the reference throughout this paper. Its cost is the motivation for
learning: the threshold search re-evaluates the capacity over every channel of a wide band
at every geometry update, on a processor that also flies the aircraft.

\subsection{Existing Learning-Based Solution: D3QN Channel Selection}
\label{sec:prior-d3qn}
Elçik \emph{et al.} \cite{elcik2025d3qn} replaced the threshold search by a learned
policy. In deep $Q$-learning \cite{mnih2015dqn}, a neural network with weights
$\boldsymbol{\theta}$ approximates the action-value function $Q(\mathbf{s},a)$, the
expected return of action $a$ in state $\mathbf{s}$, and the agent acts greedily,
$a^\star=\arg\max_a Q(\mathbf{s},a;\boldsymbol{\theta})$. Two refinements give the D3QN.
The double-DQN target \cite{vanhasselt2016double}
\begin{equation}
y=r+\beta\,Q\!\big(\mathbf{s}',\arg\max_{a'}Q(\mathbf{s}',a';\boldsymbol{\theta});
\boldsymbol{\theta}^-\big)
\label{eq:double}
\end{equation}
uses the online weights $\boldsymbol{\theta}$ to choose the next action and a separate
target network $\boldsymbol{\theta}^-$ to evaluate it, which reduces overestimation. Here
$r$ is the reward, $\mathbf{s}'$ the next state, and $\beta$ the discount factor. The
dueling architecture \cite{wang2016dueling} splits the network into a state-value stream
$V(\mathbf{s})$ and an advantage stream $A(\mathbf{s},a)$, and combines them as
\begin{equation}
Q(\mathbf{s},a)=V(\mathbf{s})+\Big(A(\mathbf{s},a)-\frac{1}{|\mathcal{A}|}
\sum_{a'\in\mathcal{A}}A(\mathbf{s},a')\Big),
\label{eq:dueling}
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{A}$ is the action set. Subtracting the mean advantage makes the
decomposition identifiable.

In \cite{elcik2025d3qn}, the state collects the path loss and the noise power of every
channel. An action $a$ selects a number of channels, and that many highest-SNR channels
are activated with EP. The reward is a logarithmic transform of the achieved capacity
(given in Section~\ref{sec:ml-d3qn}). Because the selected channels do not change the next
channel state, the problem was cast as a contextual bandit \cite{langford2007epoch}. One
network is pre-trained for each distance and total-gain pair. Over $0.5$--$1$~THz, the
learned policy matched the MaxActive capacity with about $10^4$ times fewer operations.
The present paper re-implements this policy over the full $0.75$--$4.4$~THz band of
\cite{saeed2024maxactive} and compares it with supervised alternatives
(Section~\ref{sec:ml}).

\section{ALTERNATIVE MACHINE LEARNING SOLUTIONS}
\label{sec:ml}
% ============================================================================
\subsection{Two Ways to Learn the Spectrum Allocation}
\label{sec:ml-overview}
All learned models in this work use the same physical channel state as MaxActive: for
each operating context, defined by a UAV geometry and the resulting gain $G$, beamwidth
$\psi$, and distance $d$, the per-channel path losses $\{A_i\}$ and noise powers
$\{P_{N,i}\}$ of Section~\ref{sec:channel}. Two learning targets are possible. A
\emph{capacity regressor} learns the \emph{value} of the spectrum-allocation decision: it
maps a compact feature vector $\mathbf{x}$ of the channel state to an estimate $\hat C$ of
the capacity that MaxActive would achieve, in the spirit of
\cite{sun2018learning}. A \emph{selection policy} learns the \emph{decision} itself: it maps
the channel state to a set of active channels, whose capacity is then evaluated with the
physical model \eqref{eq:snr}--\eqref{eq:capacity}. The distinction matters in practice. A
regressor returns a number, not a channel set, so it can predict a capacity that no
feasible allocation reaches. A policy returns an allocation that can be transmitted,
and the capacity reported for it is the capacity that allocation actually delivers.

Because MaxActive returns the best capacity within its search family, it upper-bounds any
model trained to imitate it. When regressor outputs are \emph{plotted} against the
reference, we therefore display
\begin{equation}
\hat{C}_{\text{clip}}=\min\!\big(\hat{C},\,C_{\text{MaxActive}}\big).
\label{eq:clip}
\end{equation}
The clip \eqref{eq:clip} requires $C_{\text{MaxActive}}$, which is exactly what a
deployed regressor does not have; if it were available online, no surrogate would be
needed. \emph{The clip is therefore used only for offline visualization.} It is not part
of the online prediction path. At deployment, the regressor emits $\hat C$ and the
policy emits a channel set, with no access to the reference. Every error reported in
Section~\ref{sec:results} is computed from raw, unclipped predictions, and the
ceiling violations that \eqref{eq:clip} would hide are reported explicitly.

\subsection{Physical Feature Vector and Learning Target}
\label{sec:features}
The regressors do not see the $|\mathcal{F}|$ individual channels. Instead, each operating
context is summarized by a $13$-dimensional feature vector built from quantities of the
channel model. The band is divided into $K=20$ equal-width sub-bands. For each sub-band
$k$, we compute the mean total path loss $\bar L_k$ (in decibels), the mean
absorption-noise power $\bar P_{N,k}$ from \eqref{eq:noise}, and a received-power indicator
$\Pi_k=\log_{10}\!\big(P_T\,G\,/\,10^{\bar L_k/10}\big)$. $\Pi_k$ is the numerator of the
SNR \eqref{eq:snr} under full power, expressed in $\log_{10}$ watts. The features
are:
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Geometry and antenna:} $\log_{10}(d+1)$ and its square; the normalized total
gain $G_{\mathrm{dB}}/60$ and its square; and the normalized beamwidth $\psi/360^\circ$.
These features capture the distance dependence of \eqref{eq:spread} and the alignment
state of \eqref{eq:gain}.
\item \emph{Channel quality across the band:} the mean and the standard deviation over
sub-bands of $\Pi_k$ and of $\log_{10}\bar P_{N,k}$; the normalized mean path loss
$\overline{L}/200$; and the fraction of sub-bands that contain channels. The means
describe how strong the link is. The standard deviations describe how frequency
selective it is, which governs how many channels are worth activating.
\item \emph{Context labels:} a mobility-scenario index and a power-allocation flag. The
allocation flag is constant within each training set, because WF and EP are learned as
separate targets.
\end{itemize}
The learning target is the base-10 logarithm of the MaxActive capacity in gigabits per
second, $y=\log_{10}(C_{\text{MaxActive}}/1~\text{Gbps})$, computed separately for WF and
EP. The logarithm is essential. Across the scenarios, the capacity spans about nine
orders of magnitude (Section~\ref{sec:results}), and on that scale the capacity curve is
smooth. For the models that are sensitive to feature scale, each feature is standardized
to zero mean and unit variance using statistics of the training data only.

\subsection{Supervised Capacity Regressors}
\label{sec:regressors}
Each regressor learns a mapping $f(\mathbf{x})\approx y$ from labeled examples
$\{(\mathbf{x}_n,y_n)\}$ generated by the reference model, using its own standard training
objective. To avoid committing to one hypothesis class in advance, we evaluate eighteen
regressors in six families \cite{pedregosa2011sklearn,chen2016xgboost}. The families,
their inductive biases, and their computational character are as follows.

\emph{Linear and regularized models} (ordinary least squares, LinearReg; Ridge; Lasso;
ElasticNet; BayesianRidge) assume that $y$ is affine in $\mathbf{x}$. In the log domain,
the capacity is approximately affine in log-distance and gain, so these models are
natural baselines. The $\ell_2$, $\ell_1$, elastic-net, and evidence-based variants
separate the bias of the affine hypothesis from the bias of the penalty. Training costs
$O(nd^2)$ for $n$ samples and $d$ features, and a prediction is a $d$-term dot product.
\emph{Robust linear regression} (Huber) uses a loss that is quadratic for small residuals
and linear for large ones, which tests sensitivity to the extreme dynamic range.

\emph{Kernel and probabilistic models} (support vector regression with a radial basis
function kernel, SVR-RBF; kernel ridge regression, KernelRidge; and a Gaussian process,
GaussProc) model smooth nonlinear functions through a kernel that measures the similarity
of two operating contexts. The Gaussian process \cite{rasmussen2006gp} uses a
squared-exponential kernel plus a white-noise term and returns the posterior mean. Its
kernel hyperparameters are set by maximizing the marginal likelihood. These models suit a
smooth capacity surface, but their training cost grows as $O(n^2)$ to $O(n^3)$ with the
number of samples.

\emph{Instance-based learning} ($k$-nearest neighbors, $k$-NN) predicts the capacity as an
average over the most similar training contexts. It interpolates locally and needs no
training, but every prediction scans the stored data.

\emph{Neural networks} (a multilayer perceptron, MLP) are flexible function approximators
trained by gradient descent on the squared error.

\emph{Tree ensembles} (RandomForest, ExtraTrees, Bagging, AdaBoost, gradient boosting,
GradBoost, and XGBoost \cite{chen2016xgboost}), together with a single DecisionTree as an
unregularized contrast, partition the feature space into regions with a constant
prediction in each. They are strong learners for tabular data, but a piecewise-constant
function cannot extrapolate a curve that spans many decades.

A histogram-based gradient-boosting regressor was also implemented and then discarded.
It is designed for large binned datasets and gives a negative cross-validated $R^2$ on
$33$ samples. Because models within a family behave alike here while the families differ
by orders of magnitude in error (Section~\ref{sec:results}), adding more members of an
existing family is unlikely to change the ranking, whereas omitting a family could.

\emph{Interpretation and limitation.} The regressor output $\hat C$ estimates the capacity
that the spectrum-allocation search would deliver at the current geometry, which can be
used, for example, in link-budget and rate decisions. A regressor does \emph{not} return
the channel set $\Omega$. Where the set itself is needed, the policy of
Section~\ref{sec:ml-d3qn} must be used. Because a regressor is not tied to a feasible
allocation, nothing prevents $\hat C$ from exceeding the physical bound; how often this
happens is a first-class evaluation criterion.

\subsection{D3QN Channel-Selection Policy}
\label{sec:ml-d3qn}
The decision branch re-implements the D3QN of \cite{elcik2025d3qn} at the same
individual-channel granularity as MaxActive.

\emph{State.} The channels are ranked by $1/(A_iP_{N,i})$, which is the SNR order
\eqref{eq:snr} at a common power level. The state stacks the log path loss and the log
noise power of the $N$ best-ranked channels:
\begin{align}
\mathbf{s}&=\big[\log_{10}A_{(1)},\dots,\log_{10}A_{(N)},\nonumber\\
&\qquad\ \log_{10}P_{N,(1)},\dots,\log_{10}P_{N,(N)}\big],
\label{eq:state}
\end{align}
where the subscript $(m)$ denotes the $m$th-ranked channel.

\emph{Action.} An action $a\in\mathcal{A}$ selects a number of channels
$N_{\text{sel}}(a)$. The $N_{\text{sel}}$ highest-ranked channels are activated with EP
\eqref{eq:ep}, so the action space contains only SNR-ordered prefixes, the same family that
MaxActive searches. The resulting allocation is evaluated with the physical model
\eqref{eq:snr}--\eqref{eq:capacity}, which gives the true capacity $C$ of the decision.

\emph{Reward.} Following \cite{elcik2025d3qn}, the reward is
\begin{equation}
r=\ln\!\big((C_{\max}-C_{\min})+1\big)-\ln\!\big((C_{\max}-C)+1\big),
\label{eq:reward}
\end{equation}
where all capacities are in bit/s. In the implementation, as in \cite{elcik2025d3qn},
$C_{\max}$ is the MaxActive-EP capacity of the current context, obtained by searching the
same SNR-ordered prefixes, and $C_{\min}=0$. The reward is therefore
$r=\ln(C_{\max}+1)-\ln(C_{\max}-C+1)$. It is zero for $C=0$ and reaches its maximum
$\ln(C_{\max}+1)$ when the policy matches the reference. The logarithms compress the very
large capacity range of a THz link. Because $C_{\max}$ enters the reward, \emph{training a
context requires the reference capacity of that context}; only the trained policy, not
its training, is independent of the search.

\emph{Network and training.} The action values are produced by the dueling decomposition
\eqref{eq:dueling} from a small fully connected trunk. Training alternates
$\epsilon$-greedy exploration, which evaluates the capacity of each explored action with
the physical model, with gradient steps that regress $Q(\mathbf{s},a)$ onto the observed
reward under a smooth-$\ell_1$ loss, using minibatches drawn from a replay memory. After
training, the returned decision is
\begin{equation}
a_{\text{out}}=\arg\max_{a\in\{a^{\star}\}\cup\mathcal{A}_{\text{exp}}} C(a),
\qquad a^{\star}=\arg\max_{a}Q(\mathbf{s},a),
\label{eq:aout}
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{A}_{\text{exp}}$ is the set of actions explored during training and $C(a)$
their evaluated capacities. That is, the greedy action of the trained network is kept
unless an action already evaluated during training delivered a higher capacity. This
fallback is part of the implementation of the policy and of every D3QN result reported
here.

\emph{This is a contextual bandit, not sequential reinforcement learning.} For a given
geometry, the channel state $\mathbf{s}$ is fixed. The agent takes one action, receives
one reward, and the episode ends. The selection made now does not change the channel state
at the next geometry, which is set by the trajectory and the physics. With no state
transition to influence, the horizon is one and the discount $\beta$ in \eqref{eq:double}
is zero. The double-DQN target therefore reduces to the immediate reward, $y=r$, and
$Q(\mathbf{s},a)$ is an estimate of the reward of action $a$. The model is a contextual
$|\mathcal{A}|$-armed bandit \cite{langford2007epoch}. The mechanisms that make deep RL
fragile, namely bootstrapping bias, moving targets, and delayed rewards, are absent.
Because the target is the immediate reward, the separate target network of
\eqref{eq:double} is not needed and is not used. We keep the dueling architecture and the
D3QN name for fidelity to \cite{elcik2025d3qn,wang2016dueling}, but claim no sequential
decision-making.

\emph{Interpretation.} The policy outputs a channel set that can be transmitted. Its
capacity is that of a feasible SNR-ordered prefix with equal power, so it cannot exceed
the best such prefix, which is the set MaxActive-EP searches for. Agreement with
MaxActive should be read with three qualifications. First, the policy can at best match
the reference; it cannot improve on it. Second, its training uses the reference capacity
$C_{\max}$ in the reward. Third, by \eqref{eq:aout}, the reported decision is the best of
the greedy action and the actions evaluated during training, so the agreement reflects the
learned network together with the exploration performed while training it. The
implementation does not record which of the two produced the returned action.

\emph{Operation at an unseen geometry, and its cost.} As in \cite{elcik2025d3qn}, the
policy is trained per operating context: one lightweight network for each
(distance, gain) pair. This is a real restriction. A previously unseen geometry is not
covered by an already-trained network, and obtaining a policy for it requires its own
training run, which itself needs the reference capacity of that geometry. That run is
short in absolute terms (Section~\ref{sec:runtime}), but it is an offline cost, and it
exceeds the cost of a single MaxActive search. The present D3QN branch therefore suits a
trajectory envelope that can be enumerated and trained for in advance, and it pays off
only when a trained context recurs. It does not suit an arbitrary geometry met in
flight. The supervised branch does not share this restriction: one fitted regressor maps
the features of any geometry inside the trained envelope to a capacity estimate. Two
routes would lift the restriction, and both are left to future work. One is to condition
a single network on the geometry descriptors that the regressors already use; the other
is to train a bank of policies offline and select among them by nearest context at run
time.

\subsection{Simulation Parameters and Data Generation}
\label{sec:setup}
\emph{Reference simulator.} The physical layer is reproduced in Python from the MATLAB
simulation code of \cite{saeed2024maxactive}. The same code supplies the LBLRTM
transmittance files, the antenna model, the initial coordinates and velocities, and the
trajectory generators. The LBLRTM files are computed for a horizontal path at $100$~m

from a figure of \cite{saeed2024maxactive}, the regenerated value is kept. The transmit
power of $24$~dBm is taken from the reference simulation code. The main parameters are
listed in Table~\ref{tab:simparams}.

\emph{Channel selection and beamwidth.} The MaxActive threshold sweep is implemented as an
equivalent sweep over the number of SNR-ranked channels that are retained: a coarse grid
is followed by a unit-step refinement around the best value. The power allocation and
MaxActive are alternated until the active set is stable. In the scenarios with beamwidth
adjustment, the common Tx/Rx beamwidth is searched over $[\psi_0,360^\circ]$ before each
selection, maximizing the EP capacity over the full band.

\emph{Mobility scenarios.} Four patterns are generated, the last three misaligned. Each
pattern is evaluated with WF and EP, and the misaligned ones with and without beamwidth
adjustment. The antenna boresights are fixed by the first position of each trajectory.
\begin{enumerate}
\def\labelenumi{\arabic{enumi})}
\item \emph{Aligned:} the Rx recedes along the $y$-axis at $10$~m/s for $11$ steps,
giving $d=1,11,\dots,101$~m with the beams aligned throughout.
\item \emph{Misaligned, diagonal mobility:} the Rx recedes with velocity
$(5\sqrt2,5\sqrt2,0)$~m/s, so the fixed $10^\circ$ beams progressively misalign; $11$
steps.
\item \emph{Misaligned, mobility over elevation:} the Rx moves on a hemisphere of radius
$20$~m around the Tx, with the elevation (zenith) angle $\phi$ swept from $0^\circ$ to
$180^\circ$ in $10^\circ$ steps ($19$ points). The distance is constant, so the capacity
change is caused only by misalignment.
\item \emph{Misaligned, real mobility traces:} the Tx and Rx positions are integrated from
measured windy-hover acceleration traces (the drone traces of \cite{moorthy2022beam}, as
supplied with the reference code), with an initial diagonal drift of the Rx. The scenario
has $11$ points over $0$--$5$~s, and the distance varies between $1.0$ and $52.9$~m.
\end{enumerate}

\emph{Datasets.} For the supervised study, one sample is built per scenario--geometry
pair. The \emph{main dataset} combines the aligned scenario and the diagonal scenario with
and without adjustment, $3\times11=33$ samples. A separate \emph{mobility dataset}
contains the elevation ($2\times19$) and real-trace ($2\times11$) points, $60$ samples, and
is built identically. Each sample carries the $13$ features of Section~\ref{sec:features}
and the targets $y$ for WF and EP. The sample count is set by the reference model rather
than by a sampling choice: the LBLRTM files resolve the straight-line geometry at eleven
ranges, and every extra sample costs a full physics evaluation. Three properties make this
small-sample regime workable. The target is a deterministic, noiseless function of the
features, so $n$ controls only the resolution of the input space, not the variance of the
labels. The input space is low dimensional and physically smooth, with capacity varying
monotonically in distance and misalignment. Finally, the evaluation protocol below is
matched to the regime. Generating more data is embarrassingly parallel and linear in
the number of operating points, at roughly \PLMASTEP{} per sample, so denser range grids,
more beamwidths, or more links would raise $n$ from tens to $10^4$--$10^6$ at a cost of
CPU-hours. The present models are validated as \emph{interpolators} within the envelope of
geometries they were trained on, with no claim of extrapolation beyond it.

\emph{Evaluation protocol.} Thirty-three samples cannot support a single train/test split
for ranking eighteen models, so all regression metrics reported for the main dataset use
\emph{nested} cross-validation (CV). On the mobility dataset, the models are tuned by the
same inner grid search and then fitted on all $60$ samples, so the errors reported there
are in-sample. An outer repeated $k$-fold loop
($5$~folds~$\times\,3$~repeats~$=15$ folds) estimates generalization. On each outer
training partition only, an inner $3$-fold grid search tunes the hyperparameters, and the
feature standardization sits inside the same pipeline, so no information leaks from the
test fold. The metrics are the root-mean-square error (RMSE) and the coefficient of
determination $R^2$ on the log target, and the \emph{median relative error} of the
capacity in linear Gbps, which avoids the inflation of the mean absolute percentage error
at the very small far-distance capacities. The hyperparameter grids are listed in
Table~\ref{tab:mlparams}. The curves plotted in the figures come from each model refit on
all samples (the estimator one would deploy), clipped at the ceiling \eqref{eq:clip} for
display, and drawn with monotone piecewise-cubic (PCHIP) interpolation in log space, with
markers at the computed points. The metrics come from raw out-of-fold predictions.

\emph{D3QN settings.} The policy of Section~\ref{sec:ml-d3qn} uses the top $N=500$ channels
in the state (a $1000$-dimensional input) and $600$ actions. The first $50$ actions select
$1$ to $50$ channels individually, and the remaining $550$ are spaced evenly up to the
full channel count. The dueling network maps $1000\!\to\!16\!\to\!8$ to a value head and a
$600$-way advantage head. It is trained for $20$ epochs of $80$ $\epsilon$-greedy actions
(Adam, learning rate $10^{-3}$, weight decay $10^{-4}$), with one network per
(distance, gain) context. One gradient step on a replay minibatch of $64$ is taken per
epoch, i.e., $20$ steps per context, while $1600$ actions are evaluated. The returned
decision follows \eqref{eq:aout}. The reference for the policy is MaxActive-EP over the
same SNR-ranked channels. Since WF and EP give nearly equal capacities
(Section~\ref{sec:results}), this comparison also tracks the WF reference.

\subsection{Results}
\label{sec:results}
\emph{Reference capacity behavior.} Table~\ref{tab:maxactive} and
Fig.~\ref{fig:maxactive} give the MaxActive capacity for the aligned and diagonal
scenarios. The ordering aligned $>$ adjusted $>$ non-adjusted holds at every distance, and
the scenarios together span about nine decades. In the aligned case the capacity decays
smoothly with distance, because both the spreading loss \eqref{eq:spread} and the
absorption loss grow with $d$ while the gain stays at its main-lobe value. Under diagonal
mobility without adjustment, the line of sight leaves the $10^\circ$ main lobe after the
first step. The link then operates on the side lobes, where the gain falls from about
$52$~dB to $\rho^2$ ($-20$~dB), and the capacity drops by five to seven orders of
magnitude relative to the aligned link. Beamwidth adjustment keeps the link in a wider
main lobe and recovers more than three of these decades. WF and EP give nearly the same capacity in every scenario. At THz
frequencies, the channels MaxActive selects already have comparable and high SNR, so
water filling adds little over equal power; this confirms the observation of
\cite{saeed2024maxactive} that MaxActive with EP approaches the WF capacity.

\emph{Prediction accuracy.} Table~\ref{tab:cv} gives the nested cross-validated metrics
for the EP target, ranked by RMSE. The WF target was cross-validated in the same way. The
two agree to within $0.055$ RMSE for every one of the eighteen models, and to within
$0.02$ for all but the single decision tree, as the near-equality of the WF and EP
capacities (Table~\ref{tab:maxactive}) implies. Only the EP column is therefore
tabulated. Three findings stand out.
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{The smooth nonparametric and kernel models perform best.} The Gaussian
process is best by a wide margin ($R^2=0.99$, median relative error $\approx1.9\%$),
followed by ExtraTrees, SVR-RBF, MLP, and $k$-NN. These models assume the smoothness that
the physical capacity surface exhibits, so they interpolate MaxActive closely and
generalize across folds.
\item \emph{Regularized linear models are stable but biased.} Ridge, Lasso, ElasticNet,
and BayesianRidge hold $R^2\approx0.97$ with small variance, yet their $\approx45\%$ median
relative error reveals a systematic offset. A model that is affine in the features cannot
capture the curvature of a capacity that decays over nine decades.
\item \emph{Cross-validation exposes failures that a single split hides.} On a single
$75/25$ split, Huber is second only to the Gaussian process (RMSE $0.183$), but under CV
it falls to $R^2=0.93\pm0.10$. Ordinary least squares looks acceptable on that split
($R^2=0.991$) but is far worse under CV ($R^2=-7.98\pm31$), dominated by unlucky folds. These two, together
with the single decision tree and gradient boosting, are the failing methods. On $33$
points a single split cannot be trusted; the nested-CV mean$\,\pm\,$std is the basis for
the ranking.
\end{itemize}

\emph{Physical-bound violations.} The last two columns of Table~\ref{tab:cv} quantify how
far the \emph{raw} predictions exceed the reference ceiling. This is what the display clip
\eqref{eq:clip} conceals, and it is the error a deployed regressor would actually commit,
since it has no ceiling to clip against. Every regressor in Table~\ref{tab:cv} exceeds the
reference at one or more points, so the size of the violation is a first-class selection
criterion. The
piecewise-constant single tree and plain gradient boosting overshoot by thousands of
percent, because they cannot extrapolate the wide dynamic range; a link budget
dimensioned on such a prediction would be badly optimistic. GaussProc and ExtraTrees, by
contrast, stay within a few percent. The D3QN capacity did not exceed the MaxActive-EP
reference at any of the evaluated geometries, as expected for a policy that selects among
the same SNR-ordered prefixes.

\emph{Scenario dependence.} Table~\ref{tab:perscen} breaks the out-of-fold median relative
error down by scenario for the strongest models. For four of the five models, at least
one misaligned scenario is harder than the aligned one; $k$-NN, for example, rises to
$20.1\%$ in the worst-SNR regime (misaligned, no adjustment). The Gaussian process is the
exception and stays within $2\%$ in all three scenarios. SVR-RBF and KernelRidge stay
within about $7\%$ everywhere, whereas ExtraTrees degrades in both misaligned scenarios. This
per-scenario stability, and not merely a low aggregate error, is what qualifies a model
as a robust predictor.

\emph{Channel-selection policy.} The D3QN, trained as a single-step contextual bandit at
every trajectory point (Section~\ref{sec:ml-d3qn}), reaches at least $99.7\%$ of the
MaxActive-EP capacity on \emph{all seven} scenario variants. The mean capacity ratio is
$99.9\%$ for the aligned case and $100.0\%$ for each of the six misaligned cases:
diagonal, elevation, and real-trace mobility, each with and without beamwidth adjustment.
The worst single operating point over all $93$ evaluated geometries ($33$ in the main
study plus $60$ mobility points) is $99.7\%$ of the reference, and no point exceeds it.
The selected channel \emph{counts} are not always identical to those of MaxActive; in the
main study they differ by up to $30$ channels (e.g., $497$ versus $467$ at $81$~m in the
aligned case). Near its maximum, the capacity is flat in the number of active channels,
so neighboring prefixes deliver almost the same capacity. These results show that a
policy operating at the same individual-channel granularity as MaxActive-EP returns a
transmittable channel set of nearly reference capacity, under elevation and real-trace
mobility as well. They must, however, be read with the qualifications of
Section~\ref{sec:ml-d3qn}. The ratio is bounded by construction, because the policy
chooses among the same SNR-ordered prefixes. Training uses the reference capacity in the
reward. The reported decision is the better of the greedy action and the best of the
$1600$ actions evaluated during training \eqref{eq:aout}. The figures above therefore
characterize the complete training-plus-selection procedure; how often the greedy
network output alone attains them was not recorded.

\emph{Discussion and limitations.} The contrast between the regressors and the policy is
the most transferable finding of this study. A regressor predicts a number and can exceed
the physical bound. The policy predicts an allocation, and the capacity it reports is the
one its choice actually delivers, so it stays at or below the MaxActive-EP reference
($99.7$--$100\%$) with no clip anywhere in the pipeline. Where an allocation must be
trusted without supervision, learning the decision is the safer target, subject to the
per-context training it currently requires. The study also has limitations that bound
these claims. The datasets are small ($33$ and $60$ points) and physically smooth, which
favors smoothness-assuming models and restricts the validated regime to interpolation
within the trained envelope. The learned models imitate a reference that is optimal only
within its threshold-search family (Section~\ref{sec:maxactive}), so they inherit that
scope rather than approximating a globally optimal allocation. The regressors predict the
capacity value rather than the channel set, so a system that needs the set must use the
policy. The policy, in turn, is trained per context rather than as a single
geometry-generalizing agent (Section~\ref{sec:ml-d3qn}).

\section{CONVERGENCE AND COMPLEXITY}
\label{sec:complexity}
% ============================================================================
\subsection{Asymptotic Cost}
MaxActive has a per-step cost of $O(Q\,|\mathcal{F}|)$ and must be re-run whenever the
geometry changes. It needs no training, but it incurs this search at every time step. The
supervised models move the cost offline. The entire nested-CV benchmark of $18$ models
over both targets completes in about $30$~s on a CPU, dominated by the grid searches, and
fitting the $18$ deployable models on the full dataset takes $0.5$~s. Afterwards, a
prediction is a single forward, kernel, or neighbor evaluation. Table~\ref{tab:complexity}
summarizes the asymptotic training and prediction complexity of representative families
($n$ samples, $d$ features, $T$ trees, $W$ network weights, $n_{sv}$ support vectors),
together with their measured cost and cross-validated accuracy, and adds the MaxActive and
D3QN references for context.

\subsection{Measured Execution Time}
\label{sec:runtime}
Asymptotic orders alone do not establish a practical benefit, so every stage was measured on
identical channel states, on a single core of an Intel Core i7-13620H, with the band
discretized into $|\mathcal{F}|=\PLNCH$ active channels per context. The measured columns
of Table~\ref{tab:complexity} average the eleven aligned contexts.

The reference costs \PLMASEL{} for the threshold sweep alone and \PLMASTEP{} for a complete
link update with power allocation iterated to convergence. On the three geometries where
both variants were timed, adding the beamwidth search raises that update from $72$~ms to
\PLMASTEPBW{}, i.e., to $1.3\times$ the fixed-beam cost. Against this, the regressor path
costs $0.84$~ms to turn the channel state into the $13$ features and $21$--$43$~$\mu$s to
evaluate a linear, kernel, or Gaussian-process model. This is \PLPREDUS{} end-to-end, an
$88\times$ reduction in the per-update decision cost. The source of this factor should be
stated precisely. The model evaluation alone is $1800$--$3700\times$ faster than the
search, but feature extraction now dominates the online path, so the end-to-end gain
saturates near two orders of magnitude, and \emph{feature extraction, not inference, is
the target for further optimization}. The tree ensembles are the exception at the other
end. They need $3.0$--$12$~ms per prediction because hundreds of estimators must each be
traversed, which cuts their advantage to $6$--$20\times$ and makes them a poor per-update
choice at this dataset size despite their accuracy. The D3QN is the fastest decision of
all. Its state is built from the SNR ranking that MaxActive computes anyway ($0.55$~ms,
shared by both), after which a forward pass and $\arg\max$ take $0.05$~ms, three orders of
magnitude below the search. It also returns the channel set itself rather than a capacity
estimate. Its cost is paid offline: $0.11$~s per context, about $4$~s for the $33$
contexts of the main study. Training one context thus costs more than one threshold
sweep (\PLMASEL{}), and it also needs that context's reference capacity
(Section~\ref{sec:ml-d3qn}). The per-context policy therefore saves computation only
when a trained context is revisited, and the online figure above applies to such
contexts.

\subsection{Memory Footprint and Airborne Deployment}
\label{sec:deploy}
For a UAV, memory and predictability matter as much as speed. The deployable models are
small: the regularized-linear models occupy $1.2$--$2.2$~KB, SVR-RBF $4.6$~KB, the Gaussian
process $17.9$~KB (its $33\times33$ kernel and training inputs), the MLP $70$~KB, and the
D3QN $21\,561$ parameters, i.e., $84$~KB in single precision. These sizes are compatible
with the on-chip memory of microcontroller-class flight companions, and all are far
smaller than the LBLRTM tables that the reference search itself must hold. Only the tree
ensembles are heavy, XGBoost at $522$~KB and ExtraTrees at $1.4$~MB, which, together with
their millisecond-scale inference, makes them a poor fit here despite their accuracy.
Three further properties favor deployment. The inference cost is constant and independent
of the data: a fixed number of multiply-accumulates with no search, iteration, or early
exit. The decision therefore has a hard worst-case latency that a threshold sweep of
state-dependent length cannot offer. All training is offline, so the platform performs no
gradient computation. Finally, the arithmetic
could be compressed further: an $8$-bit D3QN would occupy about $21$~KB (not evaluated
here), and the linear models reduce to
a $13$-element dot product. On these grounds, the Gaussian process is the better choice
where accuracy dominates ($18$~KB, $43$~$\mu$s), and the D3QN where the channel set is
needed ($84$~KB, $0.05$~ms, subject to the per-context training of
Section~\ref{sec:ml-d3qn}). The tree ensembles are best reserved for offline or
ground-station use. These figures indicate feasibility within the memory and latency
budgets of a flight companion. All timings, however, come from a laptop-class CPU, so
absolute latencies on an embedded airborne processor will be larger. The \emph{ratio}
between search and learned model is expected to carry over approximately, since both run
on the same processor, but this has not been measured. A full airborne demonstration
remains future work.

\subsection{Convergence}
For the supervised models, convergence is that of their standard solvers and is not a
limiting factor; the cost lies in the offline grid search. For the D3QN, the
single-step formulation of Section~\ref{sec:ml-d3qn} removes bootstrapping variance and
the credit-assignment horizon, which makes training well conditioned. With the budget of
$20$ epochs of $80$ actions, the returned decision \eqref{eq:aout} reached $99.7$--$100\%$
of the reference capacity in all four mobility patterns. Because \eqref{eq:aout} also
considers the actions explored during training, this budget is a measured sufficient
setting for the complete procedure, not a convergence result for the greedy network
alone. Learning curves of the greedy action were not recorded and are left to future
work. The coupled power-allocation and MaxActive loop inside the reference stops when
the two channel sets agree and is capped at $30$ iterations
(Table~\ref{tab:simparams}); its iteration counts were not recorded.

\subsection{Accuracy Versus Complexity}
A clear accuracy-versus-complexity trade-off emerges, and it is the central practical
conclusion of this study. The most accurate model, the Gaussian process, has the highest
asymptotic training cost ($O(n^3)$ time, $O(n^2)$ memory, and a prediction that requires
the full training kernel). The next best, SVR-RBF and kernel ridge, are likewise kernel based at
$O(n^2\!-\!n^3)$, and the cheapest models ($O(nd^2)$ training, $O(d)$ prediction) are
systematically biased ($\approx45\%$ median error). The accuracy ranking of
Table~\ref{tab:cv} therefore roughly tracks the asymptotic cost ranking of
Table~\ref{tab:complexity}: the last few percent of accuracy are bought at a
super-linear price in the number of samples.

Two regimes invert the recommendation, however. At the present scale ($n\le60$), the
asymptotic penalty is invisible in wall-clock terms. The Gaussian process trains in
$14$~ms and predicts in $43$~$\mu$s, which is cheaper at inference than $k$-NN or any
ensemble. The most accurate model can therefore be chosen without a practical penalty, and
the Gaussian process is the model we recommend at this scale. What costs time here is the
number of estimators rather than the asymptotic class, which is why the tree ensembles are
the slowest predictors in the table. As $n$ grows toward the $10^4$--$10^6$ samples of
Section~\ref{sec:setup}, the cubic training cost and quadratic memory of the kernel
methods become prohibitive, while the ensembles amortize their per-tree overhead, and the
balance shifts to XGBoost and ExtraTrees. A reasonable guideline is therefore to prefer
kernel methods while the dataset is small and tree ensembles once it is not.

\section{CONCLUSIONS}
\label{sec:conc}
% ============================================================================
This correspondence studied spectrum allocation for THz UAV links, where molecular
absorption, absorption noise, and mobility-induced beam misalignment make the choice of
active channels and their powers decisive for capacity. Taking the MaxActive threshold
search as the reference solution, we investigated two machine-learning alternatives that
work from the same physical channel state: supervised regressors that predict the
capacity the search achieves, and a D3QN contextual-bandit policy that learns the
channel selection itself.

Three findings carry beyond this system. First, the inductive bias that matters is
smoothness. The capacity surface is smooth and wide-ranging, so kernel and
Gaussian-process models track the reference closely, whereas piecewise-constant learners
cannot extrapolate it and overshoot the physical bound by orders of magnitude. Second, on
datasets this small, the evaluation protocol decides the ranking. A single split reorders
the models and hides overfitting, so nested cross-validation is a requirement rather than
a refinement. Third, learning the \emph{decision} is safer than learning its
\emph{value}. A policy that selects channels reports only capacities that a transmittable
allocation actually delivers, whereas a value regressor can predict a rate the link
cannot support.

In terms of cost, once trained, both learned branches lower the online decision cost by
about two orders of magnitude or more relative to the search, and they fit in the memory
of a small flight computer. For the regressors, feature extraction rather than inference
now limits the speed-up. At the present scale, the Gaussian process is the preferred
capacity predictor, and the D3QN is preferred where the channel set is needed. Only as the
dataset grows by orders of magnitude does the super-linear cost of kernel methods favor
tree ensembles. Three caveats bound these conclusions. First, the D3QN branch is trained
per operating context with the reference capacity in its reward, and its reported
decision also draws on the actions explored in training; it therefore covers a
pre-enumerated trajectory envelope rather than an arbitrary unseen geometry, and it pays
off only when trained contexts recur. Second, the regressors are validated as
interpolators within the trained envelope. Third, all timings come from a laptop-class
CPU. Future work will replace the per-context bandits with a single geometry-generalizing
policy, evaluate the greedy policy without the training-time fallback, predict the channel
set rather than only the capacity, validate on embedded hardware, and extend the dataset
into the regime where the complexity trade-off binds.
